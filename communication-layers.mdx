<Card title="Definition" type="note" horizontal>
  "Social Penetration Theory" = the idea that relationships develop as people gradually share more about themselves, moving from surface-level information to more personal thoughts and feelings. It was introduced by psychologists Irwin Altman and Dalmas A. Taylor in 1973 in their book _Social Penetration: The Development of Interpersonal Relationships_. 
</Card>

## The Onion Model

### Outer Layers

<Columns cols={2}>
  <Column>
    <Card title="Definition" type="note">
      "Outer Layers" = the superficial, public level of communication in which people share observable characteristics and safe, basic information, such as their appearance, hobbies, role, or general preferences.
    </Card>

    <Card title="How we encourage communication:" icon="sparkles">
      - We give new hires a short list of colleagues to meet, with a specific reason for each introduction.
      - We ask people to include their role and current project in introductions, rather than recite their job title.
      - We pair new employees with a colleague outside their immediate team for their first month.
      - We make room before workshops for people who have not met to introduce themselves.
      - We rotate seats at in-person team lunches so people can meet beyond their usual group.
      - We organize short project demonstrations where employees can ask each other basic questions about their work.
      - We ask meeting hosts to introduce unfamiliar participants and explain why they are there.
    </Card>
  </Column>
  <Column>
    <Card title="What builds trust:" type="check">
      - Greeting people warmly and by name
      - Being consistently polite and reliable in small things
      - Showing genuine interest in basic facts about colleagues
      - Remembering details from earlier chats
      - Sharing one's own basic background
      - Matching the other person's level of formality
    </Card>

    <Card title="What destroys trust:" type="danger">
      - Ignoring or dismissing colleagues
      - Forgetting names or details repeatedly
      - Forming cliques that leave newcomers out
      - Using sarcasm or put-downs as "humor"
      - Asking questions but not listening
      - Gossiping about others' basic details
    </Card>
  </Column>
</Columns>

### Middle Layers

<Columns cols={2}>
  <Column>
    <Card title="Definition" type="note">
      "Middle Layers" = the moderately personal level of communication in which people share opinions, preferences, goals, attitudes, and everyday experiences after a basic level of trust has developed.
    </Card>

    <Card title="How we encourage communication:" icon="sparkles">
      - We ask project partners to tell each other how they prefer to receive feedback before work begins.
      - We encourage colleagues to say, “I see it differently because…” instead of silently agreeing.
      - We give teams time to discuss what they enjoyed and disliked about a project, not just whether it met its target.
      - We pair employees from different functions to compare how the same decision affects their work.
      - We encourage people to tell colleagues when their priorities conflict rather than quietly delaying a request.
      - We use project retrospectives to let peers compare their different experiences of the same event.
      - We ask colleagues to discuss what kind of work they want to learn, so they can offer each other opportunities.
      - We normalize saying, “I’m not convinced yet,” before a decision is final.
    </Card>
  </Column>
  <Column>
    <Card title="What builds trust:" type="check">
      - Matching the depth of what others share instead of overshooting
      - Disagreeing with the idea, not the person
      - Crediting ideas publicly and by name
      - Admitting small mistakes without excuses
      - Keeping opinions shared in one-to-ones private
      - Giving specific, actionable feedback
      - Responding with curiosity to unpopular views
      - Letting people share at their own pace
    </Card>

    <Card title="What destroys trust:" type="danger">
      - Mocking or dismissing an opinion
      - Repeating private comments to others
      - Sharing much more than others are ready for
      - Staying silent while others open up
      - Punishing honest disagreement, formally or socially
      - Criticizing people in front of the group
      - Pressing people to reveal their views
    </Card>
  </Column>
</Columns>

### Inner Layers

<Columns cols={2}>
  <Column>
    <Card title="Definition" type="note">
      "Inner Layers" = the private and emotionally significant level of communication in which people disclose personal values, fears, sensitive experiences, close relationships, and feelings not usually shared with strangers.
    </Card>

    <Card title="How we encourage communication:" icon="sparkles">
      - We teach colleagues to ask privately, “Would you like to talk about what’s making this difficult?” rather than asking in a meeting.
      - We make it acceptable to tell a trusted colleague, “I’m overwhelmed and need help prioritizing.”
      - We encourage peers to discuss tension between them directly before resentment builds.
      - We make private peer mentoring available for discussing setbacks, doubts, and difficult decisions.
      - We encourage colleagues to share what a missed deadline or sharp comment meant to them, not just debate the facts.
      - We support a colleague who says, “I’m dealing with something personal,” without asking for the details.
      - We help peers discuss when a working arrangement feels unfair without attacking each other’s character.
    </Card>
  </Column>
  <Column>
    <Card title="What builds trust:" type="check">
      - Responding to mistakes and worries with support before solutions
      - Keeping sensitive disclosures strictly confidential
      - Sharing one's own struggles when it's appropriate and voluntary
      - Following up later on what someone confided
      - Speaking up for colleagues when they aren't present
      - Respecting a "no" when someone doesn't want to share
      - Thanking people for their openness
    </Card>

    <Card title="What destroys trust:" type="danger">
      - Breaking confidence, even "accidentally"
      - Using someone's disclosed weakness in evaluations or arguments
      - Reacting with ridicule, shock or indifference
      - Brushing off worries ("everyone has problems")
      - Requiring emotional exposure, such as mandatory vulnerability exercises
      - Dumping your own problems without regard for the listener
      - Withdrawing after someone opens up
    </Card>
  </Column>
</Columns>

### Core Layers

<Columns cols={2}>
  <Column>
    <Card title="Definition" type="note">
      "Core Layers" = the deepest and most protected level of communication in which people reveal their central identity, fundamental beliefs, deeply held values, strongest emotions, and most vulnerable aspects of themselves.
    </Card>

    <Card title="How we encourage communication:" icon="sparkles">
      - We make it clear that a private disclosure does not become permission to ask further personal questions.
      - We encourage colleagues to ask what support would be useful instead of guessing from someone’s identity or history.
      - We teach people not to share another colleague’s personal story, even with someone they believe would be sympathetic.
      - We give employees a way to say, “This matters deeply to me, but I don’t want to discuss the details.”
      - We ask colleagues not to turn someone’s vulnerable disclosure into a lesson for the rest of the team.
      - We teach people to respond to a disclosure with “Thank you for trusting me,” rather than praise it as “brave” in front of others.
      - We protect an employee’s right to be known through their work and everyday interactions without revealing their deepest private self.
    </Card>
  </Column>
  <Column>
    <Card title="What builds trust:" type="check">
      - Treating what people keep private as a legitimate choice
      - Proving long-term consistency and proven discretion
      - Offering support without demanding explanations
      - Staying honest even when it's uncomfortable
      - Accepting people as they are, not as their role
      - Setting an example of healthy boundaries
    </Card>

    <Card title="What destroys trust:" type="danger">
      - Leaking deeply private information
      - Withdrawing support after disclosure
      - Using private knowledge as leverage
      - Ignoring signs that someone wants privacy
      - Expecting private information because of closeness
    </Card>
  </Column>
</Columns>

### Breadth Dimension

<Columns cols={2}>
  <Column>
    <Card title="Definition" type="note">
      "Breadth Dimension" = the range and number of different topics, areas of life, or personal categories that people discuss and disclose during communication.
    </Card>

    <Card title="How we encourage more breadth:" icon="sparkles">
    </Card>
  </Column>
  <Column>
    <Card title="Benefits of more breadth:" type="check">
      - It helps people find common ground faster.
      - It makes conversation easier, with more topics to draw on.
      - It makes relationships more resilient when one topic runs dry.
      - It speeds up trust-building through repeated positive exchanges.
      - It reduces awkwardness in informal settings such as breaks, events.
      - It helps newcomers feel included sooner.
      - It creates a stronger base for deeper communication later.
    </Card>
  </Column>
</Columns>

### Depth Dimension

<Columns cols={2}>
  <Column>
    <Card title="Definition" type="note">
      "Depth Dimension" = the level of intimacy, personal meaning, and emotional sensitivity of information shared, from general public facts to deeply private thoughts and feelings.
    </Card>

    <Card title="How we encourage more depth:" icon="sparkles">
    </Card>
  </Column>
  <Column>
    <Card title="Benefits of more depth:" type="check">
      - It increases psychological safety, so people can admit mistakes.
      - It creates a sense of being known and accepted, not just tolerated.
      - It strengthens loyalty and commitment to colleagues and the team.
      - It makes honest feedback easier to give and receive.
      - It helps people speak up about problems before they grow.
      - It improves understanding of what motivates each person.
      - It makes support more useful, because it fits the person's real needs.
      - It encourages sharing of ideas that feel risky or unfinished.
    </Card>
  </Column>
</Columns>
